Bookkeeping Proposal

Clean books — and a P&L you can actually run the restaurant on.

Prepared for Janice · The Rock Wood Fired Pizza Prepared by Dan Cope September 21, 2026

Where you're starting

A strong restaurant, with a stack that doesn't land in QuickBooks.

The Rock at Belmar runs roughly $250,000 a month in gross sales — about $3 million a year — as a full-service kitchen and bar. Since taking it over on December 15, 2025, you've rebuilt the operating side yourself: inventory and recipes into Toast, scheduling onto Sling, invoice capture onto xtraCHEF, payroll kept in-house on Alliance, liquor through Fintech. That's the hard part, and it's largely done.

What hasn't been rebuilt is the last mile — the part where all of that lands in QuickBooks as numbers you can read. Nine months in, nothing is split out: a month's P&L can show rent at nearly double what you actually pay, and a thirty-day view can show a $3 million restaurant roughly breaking even. Those aren't small errors to hunt down one at a time. They're the symptom of a restaurant stack that was never connected properly on the accounting side.

This isn't a question of effort or care. Restaurants break accounting in specific, predictable ways — and every one of them lives in a system your books have to be wired into. That's the work.

What I'll look at first

Four places this almost always goes wrong — and where I'd start.

01
The Belmar PIF
Belmar carries a 2.5% Public Improvement Fee. It's a fee, not a tax — it's remitted to the district rather than the city, so it belongs in a liability account, never in sales. At your volume that's roughly $6,000 a month that shouldn't be sitting in revenue. It also has to be stated separately from sales tax, and because it forms part of the sale price it is itself taxable.
02
The sales tax rate inside Belmar
Lakewood reduces its city sales tax from 3% to 1% inside the PIF district while the fee is in effect — so Belmar's combined rate is materially lower than the rest of Lakewood. First check is simply what Toast is configured to charge, and whether it matches. I'll confirm the current rate directly with the city and the district.
03
Third-party delivery fees aren't in the books
DoorDash sales ring in Toast at full menu price, but the money arrives days later net of commission. Without a journal entry bridging the two, sales read low and the commission never appears as an expense at all — which quietly distorts food cost and margin every single month.
04
Payroll and tips landing as lump sums
If Alliance's general ledger file isn't mapped into QuickBooks, payroll enters as bank drafts — no kitchen-versus-front-of-house split, no employer tax detail, and no prime cost. And because tips pay out bi-weekly through payroll, a real tips balance sits on the books between runs. It has to clear through a liability, or revenue and labor are both wrong at once.

Scope of work

Wire it up properly, then keep it clean.

  • Onboarding and setup, one time. Build a proper restaurant chart of accounts, move you to QuickBooks Plus and set up class tracking for dine-in, bar, takeout and delivery, and connect Toast, xtraCHEF, Fintech and Alliance so the data flows in on its own. Includes a review of February through September 2026, the PIF and sales-tax check above, and a look at the opening balance sheet from the December purchase — equipment, leasehold, franchise fee and the acquisition loan.
  • Every month from October 1. Categorize and reconcile every account, post sales from Toast rather than from deposits, bridge the DoorDash fees, clear tips through a liability, book payroll from Alliance's mapped file, and close the month with financials you can use — plus both sales-tax returns, state and city.
  • The rolling P&L pack, every month. The report you've been rebuilding by hand: each period across, with a percentage-of-gross-sales column beside it, so food, beer and wine, produce and labor are all readable as a percentage — and prime margin is a number you check, not a number you calculate. QuickBooks can't produce this across multiple periods on its own, so I build it and send it.
  • In step with your accountant. I own the monthly books and the reporting; your accountant keeps the tax return — now working from numbers that tie out. You keep running payroll exactly as you do today.
