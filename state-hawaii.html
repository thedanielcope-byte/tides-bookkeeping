Hawaii's Economy & Business Climate

Hawaii is one of the most unique places in America to run a business. Its economy runs on visitors, the U.S. military, diversified agriculture, healthcare, and government — with a rising wave of remote workers and tech founders who now call the islands home. That opportunity comes with a real cost of doing business: high shipping and real-estate costs, and a tax structure found nowhere else in the country.

The biggest difference for local bookkeeping is the General Excise Tax (GET). Hawaii has no sales tax. Instead, the state levies GET on nearly all gross business income — about 4% statewide, and 4.5% on Oʻahu with the county surcharge. It applies to services and rents, not just goods, and it's owed on gross receipts whether or not you passed it on to your customer. Tracking GET liability and staying ready for periodic filings and the annual G-49 reconciliation is a recurring need for every Hawaii business.

🧾
General Excise Tax (GET) — Hawaii's Signature
Not a sales tax — a gross-receipts tax on almost all business activity, including services and rents. Roughly 4% statewide (4.5% on Oʻahu). Clean books make GET filings and the annual G-49 reconciliation far simpler.
🌴
Tourism — The Economic Engine
Waikīkī, the Kona and Kohala coasts, South Maui, and Kauaʻi draw millions of visitors a year — driving hotels, restaurants, tours, retail, and a huge vacation-rental market across the islands.
🪖
Military — Pearl Harbor & Beyond
Joint Base Pearl Harbor-Hickam, Schofield Barracks, Marine Corps Base Hawaii at Kāneʻohe Bay, and Kauaʻi's PMRF anchor a defense economy that supports thousands of contractors and small businesses.
🌾
Diversified Agriculture
World-famous Kona coffee, macadamia nuts, tropical fruit and orchids, taro, and cattle ranching in Waimea — Hawaii's ag sector has diversified far beyond the old sugar and pineapple plantations.
💻
Remote Work & Tech — A Growing Sector
Kakaʻako's innovation district, Mililani Tech Park, and the Maui Research & Technology Park anchor a growing base of startups, data operations, and remote professionals reshaping the island economy.

Key Highway Infrastructure

Hawaii's road network is island-by-island — the H-series interstates on Oʻahu, plus the belt highways that ring each of the other islands.

H-1 — Oʻahu's Main Artery
Runs from Kapolei through Honolulu to the east side — the busiest freeway in the state and the spine of Oʻahu commerce and commuting.
H-2 & H-3 — Central & Windward Oʻahu
H-2 climbs to Central Oʻahu (Mililani, Wahiawā); H-3 tunnels through the Koʻolau range to connect Honolulu with Kāneʻohe and windward bases.
Kamehameha & Pali/Likelike Hwys
The Pali and Likelike highways cross the Koʻolau to windward Kailua and Kāneʻohe, while Kamehameha Hwy rings much of Oʻahu's coastline.
Queen Kaʻahumanu Hwy — Kona
The main west-side corridor on Hawaiʻi Island, linking Kailua-Kona's resorts, the airport at Keāhole, and the Kohala coast.
Hana Hwy & Piʻilani Hwy — Maui
The Hana Hwy links Kahului across Maui, while the Piʻilani Hwy runs the South Maui corridor through Kīhei and Wailea.
Kuhio & Kaumualiʻi Hwys — Kauaʻi
Kauaʻi's two belt highways run north and west out of Līhuʻe — the arteries connecting the Garden Isle's towns and resorts.

The Big Island's summit observatories and Hilo's ʻImiloa Astronomy Center make Hawaiʻi Island a global hub for astronomy and science support work.
