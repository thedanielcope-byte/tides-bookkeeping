What makes restaurant bookkeeping different

A restaurant isn't just a small business that sells food — it's high-volume, low-margin, and cash-and-card intensive, with costs that move every single day. Your books have to track daily sales from the POS, food and beverage cost of goods sold, tips, comps and voids, and third-party delivery fees, then reconcile all of it against deposits and payment processors. Prime cost: the number that runs your restaurant

Prime cost — cost of goods sold plus total labor, as a percentage of sales — is the one number every operator should watch. Those are your two biggest, most controllable costs, and most healthy full-service restaurants keep prime cost around 60–65% of sales. Books that surface it every period let you fix a bad food or labor week before it becomes a bad quarter.

Restaurant bookkeeping FAQ

Restaurants run on razor-thin margins and high transaction volume, so the books track things most businesses never touch: daily sales from a POS, food and beverage cost of goods sold, tips and gratuities, comps and voids, third-party delivery fees, and prime cost. Sales come in every day and have to be reconciled against the POS, deposits, and payment processors — not just categorized once a month. Getting these right is the difference between books that tell you your true food cost and books that just track your bank account.
Prime cost is your cost of goods sold (food and beverage) plus total labor cost, expressed as a percentage of sales. It's the single most important number in a restaurant because those two lines are the largest and most controllable costs you have. Most healthy full-service restaurants aim to keep prime cost around 60–65% of sales; when it drifts higher, profit disappears fast. Good bookkeeping surfaces prime cost every period so you can act before a bad month becomes a bad quarter.
It depends on size and complexity. QuickBooks handles a single-location restaurant's core accounting fine, especially paired with a bookkeeper who knows the industry. Restaurant365 is a purpose-built platform that combines accounting with inventory, recipe and menu costing, scheduling, and POS integrations — it earns its cost once you're managing multiple locations, tight food-cost control, or heavy inventory. Many owners start on QuickBooks and move to Restaurant365 as they scale.
Outsourced monthly restaurant bookkeeping generally runs a few hundred dollars a month, scaling with your number of locations, sales volume, POS and payroll complexity, and whether you're on QuickBooks or Restaurant365. That's a fraction of a full-time in-house bookkeeper, and far less than the cost of flying blind on food and labor cost. If you're behind, catch-up is priced separately based on how many months need reconstructing.
Tips have to be tracked carefully because they affect payroll, taxes, and your reported labor cost. Credit-card tips flow through your POS and payment processor and are typically paid out to staff, while cash tips are reported by employees. The books need to separate tips from restaurant revenue, record tip payouts correctly, and support tip reporting requirements (including IRS Form 8027 for large food-and-beverage establishments). Mishandled tips are one of the most common restaurant bookkeeping errors.
Restaurants should reconcile daily sales continuously and close the books on a set period — many use a weekly or four-week accounting period rather than a calendar month, because it keeps day-of-week patterns consistent. At minimum, sales, deposits, and payment processors should be reconciled every week and a full close done every period, so prime cost and P&L are current enough to actually manage from.
