The Best QuickBooks Live Alternatives in 2026

T Tides Bookkeeping · August 1, 2026 · 9 min read

QuickBooks Live is a reasonable product — but it isn't the right fit for everyone. Maybe you've hit the limits of its team-and-video-call model, maybe your industry has mechanics it doesn't handle well, or maybe you just want one accountable person who knows your business. Whatever the reason, here are the real alternatives in 2026, honestly compared — including where we'd steer you away from us. (If you're still deciding whether to leave at all, start with our head-to-head: QuickBooks Live vs. a dedicated bookkeeper.)

First, what are you actually replacing?

QuickBooks Live comes in two very different tiers, and the right alternative depends on which one you're leaving:

  • Expert Assisted (~$50/month) is an advice line — you keep the books, they answer questions. If that's what you have, your "alternative" is really just a different DIY support option (or leveling up to full service).
  • Full-Service Bookkeeping (~$300–$700/month) is a done-for-you service. This is what most people mean by "a QuickBooks Live alternative," so it's the focus below.

Keep one principle in mind as you shop: whoever you choose, your books should live in your QuickBooks Online (or Xero) subscription, so your data stays portable if the relationship ever ends. That single choice is what makes any future switch painless.

1. A dedicated remote bookkeeping firm

Best for: owners who want one accountable person, industry-specific know-how, and room to grow into payroll, AP/AR, or catch-up work.

This is the closest thing to "QuickBooks Live, but with a real bookkeeper attached." A remote firm works inside your own QuickBooks Online file, assigns you a dedicated bookkeeper by name, and delivers clean monthly financials — but unlike the Intuit pool, that person knows your history and your industry, and the scope flexes as you grow. Pricing is comparable: dedicated flat-rate firms (ours included) typically run $300–$1,000/month by volume, the same territory as QB Live's full-service tiers. This is what Tides does, so weigh our take accordingly — but the structural advantage (a person, not a queue) is real regardless of who you pick.

2. A local bookkeeper or small firm

Best for: owners who value in-person contact or deep local tax knowledge, and don't mind hunting for capacity.

A good local bookkeeper gives you a face, a handshake, and hometown context. The trade-offs: quality varies enormously, the best ones are often full and not taking clients, and "local" increasingly means "remote with a local feel" anyway — most bookkeeping is done through the same cloud software no matter where the bookkeeper sits. If you go this route, vet carefully; our guide to finding a bookkeeper you can trust covers the questions to ask and the red flags to walk away from.

3. Another app-based service (Bench, Pilot, Bookkeeper360, etc.)

Best for: owners who liked the software-first, packaged feel of QuickBooks Live and want a similar experience elsewhere.

Several venture-backed platforms offer a QB-Live-style experience — software-first, priced by package. The good ones are genuinely good. But read the structure carefully: some keep your books in their proprietary platform rather than your own QuickBooks file, which is exactly the risk that burned 12,000 businesses when Bench abruptly shut down in December 2024 (it was acquired and restarted, but the lesson stands). Ask two questions before signing: whose software are my books in, and what happens to them if I leave?

4. A fractional CPA or accounting firm

Best for: businesses that need tax strategy and advisory alongside the bookkeeping, and can pay for it.

A CPA firm bundles bookkeeping with tax planning, entity strategy, and higher-level advisory. That's real value if you need it — but you'll pay CPA rates for work a bookkeeper can do at a fraction of the cost, and many CPA firms outsource the actual bookkeeping anyway. For most small businesses, the efficient setup is a dedicated bookkeeper for the monthly work plus a CPA at tax time; our breakdown of bookkeeper vs. CPA vs. accountant explains who does what.

5. DIY with QuickBooks Online (plus the ~$50 advice tier)

Best for: young, low-volume businesses with simple books and a few disciplined hours a month.

Completely viable early on. QuickBooks Online (or Xero) plus a little discipline handles a simple service business fine, and if you want a safety net, QuickBooks Live's $50/month Expert Assisted tier is a cheap way to get a professional sanity-check without full service. The honest question is opportunity cost: once you're spending 8–10 hours a month wrestling with categorization, you're paying your most valuable time for your lowest-value task. Our seven signs it's time to hire a bookkeeper maps where that line usually is.

6. Hire an in-house bookkeeper

Best for: larger businesses with enough volume and complexity to keep someone busy full-time.

At real scale, an employee makes sense — full control, full availability. But for most small businesses it's overkill: salary, benefits, payroll taxes, software, and management overhead for a role that a remote firm covers for a few hundred a month. The math only works once your transaction volume genuinely fills a full-time seat, which is later than most owners assume.

How to choose

Match the alternative to why you're leaving. Left because you wanted a real person → a dedicated remote firm or a good local bookkeeper. Left because the scope was too narrow → a dedicated firm that does AP/AR, payroll, and catch-up. Left because it was too expensive for what you needed → drop to the $50 advice tier and DIY. Left because you need tax strategy too → a fractional CPA, or a bookkeeper plus a CPA at tax time.

Whatever you choose, price the two pieces separately: ongoing monthly service, and any one-time catch-up if you've fallen behind.
